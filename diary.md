# Diary

## Week 1

### Day 1 (May 25, Mon)
The first day of training began with a comprehensive **course introduction** that outlined the objectives and structure of the program. We were introduced to the **USB microphone system**, learning about its design, working principle, and how signals are captured and processed. This gave us a practical perspective on audio systems and their applications. The session then shifted to **Linux and Git**, two essential tools for engineers. Linux provided exposure to command-line operations, while Git introduced us to version control, which is critical for collaborative development. To assess our baseline knowledge, a **basic circuit quiz** was conducted, covering Ohm’s law, resistive networks, and voltage-current relationships. This helped us identify strengths and areas needing improvement. Overall, the day was a balanced mix of hardware introduction, software tools, and theoretical assessment, ensuring we were well-prepared for the deeper technical sessions ahead.


### Day 2 (May 26, Tue)
The second day of training focused on the fundamentals of linear circuit analysis. We studied key concepts including Kirchhoff’s Current Law (KCL), Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law (KVL), Superposition Theorem, Node and Mesh Analysis, Thevenin’s Theorem, Norton’s Theorem, and the Maximum Power Transfer Theorem. These principles are essential for analyzing and simplifying electrical circuits. The practical session involved using Ngspice and Xschem to simulate circuits such as voltage dividers and MOSFET ID–VGS characteristics using the Level-1 model. The simulations helped reinforce the theoretical concepts by providing a clear understanding of circuit behavior and device characteristics. Overall, the day effectively combined analytical techniques with hands-on simulation experience, strengthening our foundation in circuit analysis and design.


### Day 3 (May 27, Wed)
The third day was dedicated to **RC circuit transient analysis**. We explored how capacitors charge and discharge in response to step inputs, and how the time constant (\(\tau\)) influences circuit behavior. Several problems were solved to understand the mathematical modeling of these transients. The session then moved to **active OpAmp circuits**, where we studied amplifiers, integrators, and differentiators. These circuits are widely used in analog signal processing and form the basis of many real-world applications. In the **Ngspice lab**, we simulated RC circuits to observe their step responses and pulse train behavior, especially when RC values were much larger than the time constant. This practical exercise highlighted how theoretical equations translate into real-world waveforms. The day concluded with a deeper appreciation of how passive and active components interact to shape circuit responses.

### Day 4(May 28,Thrus)
The fourth day of training focused on the frequency response of linear circuits, with an emphasis on high-pass and low-pass filters. We learned how these filters selectively allow or attenuate signals based on their frequency and explored their significance in signal processing, communication systems, and electronic circuit design. The theoretical session included the analysis of both passive RC filters and active filter circuits, providing insight into their operating principles and frequency characteristics. During the laboratory session, we were introduced to the ADALM2000, a multifunctional hardware instrument used for circuit testing and measurement. We learned how to configure the device, perform signal measurements, and analyze circuit responses experimentally. This hands-on experience reinforced the theoretical concepts by demonstrating the practical implementation and testing of frequency-dependent circuits. Overall, the day enhanced our understanding of filter design, frequency response, and the use of modern electronic measurement tools

### Day 5 (May 29, Fri)
The fifth day focused on the **Thevenin equivalent of the MEMS microphone**. We studied how complex systems can be simplified into equivalent circuits for easier analysis. This was followed by **modeling the Analog Front-End (AFE) of the amplifier**, which is critical in audio systems for signal conditioning. The lab session continued with the **ADALM 2000**, where we performed practical experiments to validate our theoretical models. This exercise demonstrated how microphones and amplifiers interact, and how equivalent circuit models help in predicting system behavior. The day emphasized the importance of abstraction in engineering, showing how complex devices can be represented with simpler models without losing accuracy. By the end of the session, we had a strong grasp of both theoretical modeling and practical validation using modern lab tools.

## Week 2

### Day 6 (June 1, Mon)
The sixth day of training was primarily focused on laboratory exercises and practical implementation. We began by modeling the MEMS microphone and its Analog Front-End (AFE) to understand how acoustic signals are converted into electrical signals and subsequently amplified and conditioned for further processing. The session included analyzing equivalent circuit models and simulating their behavior to gain deeper insight into the operation of the microphone system. We also continued working with the ADALM2000, learning how to configure the hardware, connect measurement probes, and perform signal analysis. Practical experiments on voltage divider circuits and Thevenin equivalent circuits further reinforced the theoretical concepts covered in previous sessions. Overall, the day provided valuable hands-on experience in circuit modeling, simulation, and experimental verification, strengthening our understanding of MEMS-based audio systems and analog circuit design.

### Day 7 (June 2, Tue)
On the seventh day, we were introduced to the **Integrated Circuit (IC) design flow**. This session explained the step-by-step process of designing ICs, starting from specification, schematic design, simulation, layout, verification, and finally fabrication. Understanding this flow is crucial for anyone aspiring to work in semiconductor industries. The lecture emphasized how each stage requires precision and collaboration across multiple teams. In the lab, we conducted **microphone and AFE testing and characterization**. This involved measuring parameters such as gain, bandwidth, and noise levels, and comparing them against theoretical predictions. We also learned how to identify discrepancies between modeled behavior and actual hardware performance. This day highlighted the importance of characterization in validating designs and ensuring reliability. By the end of the session, we had a clear picture of how IC design principles are applied to real devices like microphones and amplifiers.

### Day 8(June 3,wed)
The eighth day of training focused on passive electronic devices, including resistors, capacitors, and inductors, which serve as the fundamental building blocks of electronic circuits. We studied their construction, operating principles, electrical characteristics, and behavior under different operating conditions, with particular emphasis on their frequency-dependent response. The session also explored the applications of passive components in circuits such as filters, timing networks, voltage dividers, and impedance matching systems. In addition, we discussed how passive devices interact with active components to achieve the desired circuit performance in both analog and digital designs. Although no laboratory session was conducted, the theoretical discussions provided a strong foundation for understanding the role of passive components in practical circuit design. Overall, the day strengthened our knowledge of the fundamental elements that form the basis of more advanced electronic systems.

### Day 9 (June 4, Thu)
The ninth day focused on **Semiconductor IC Devices**, with particular emphasis on **MOS devices**. The lecture began with an overview of semiconductor physics, explaining how materials like silicon form the foundation of modern integrated circuits. We studied the behavior of PN junctions, diodes, and transistors, gradually moving toward MOSFETs, which are the cornerstone of digital and analog IC design. The session highlighted the importance of threshold voltage, channel formation, and scaling effects in MOS devices. Following this, we revisited the **IC design process**, reinforcing the stages of specification, schematic design, simulation, layout, verification, and fabrication. This provided a holistic view of how semiconductor devices are transformed into functional ICs. The day also included a **Siliwiz assignment**, where we applied theoretical knowledge to practical modeling tasks. Siliwiz allowed us to simulate semiconductor behavior, bridging the gap between device physics and circuit-level applications. By the end of the day, we had a deeper appreciation of how MOS devices underpin the IC design flow and how simulation tools accelerate learning and design validation.


### Day 10
The tenth day of training focused on MOS transistor parameter extraction and device modeling through laboratory experiments. We learned how to extract important MOSFET parameters, including threshold voltage (VTH), transconductance (gm), and subthreshold characteristics, from experimental measurements. Using the ADALM2000 and Scopy, we performed electrical measurements on MOS transistor circuits and analyzed the collected data to understand device behavior under different operating conditions. The extracted parameters were then incorporated into SPICE models to simulate transistor performance accurately, highlighting the relationship between experimental results and circuit simulation. In addition, reference material based on Kang et al. provided deeper insights into MOSFET operation, device scaling, and modeling techniques used in integrated circuit design. Overall, the session enhanced our understanding of semiconductor device characterization, parameter extraction, and SPICE-based modeling, providing valuable practical experience in transistor-level analysis and simulation.

### Day 11 (June 8, Mon)
The eleventh day began with a detailed study of *MOS parameters and I-V characteristics*. We explored the fundamental operation of MOSFETs, including cutoff, triode, and saturation regions. The lecture emphasized how threshold voltage, mobility, and oxide capacitance influence device behavior. We plotted I-V curves to visualize how drain current varies with gate voltage and drain-source voltage, reinforcing the theoretical equations. This session provided the foundation for understanding transistor operation in both analog and digital circuits. In the lab, we measured MOSFET characteristics using Scopy and ADALM 2000, validating theoretical models with practical data. By the end of the day, we had a strong grasp of how MOSFET parameters define circuit performance and how to interpret experimental I-V plots.


### Day 12
The twelfth day of training focused on the design and analysis of current mirrors, which are fundamental building blocks in analog integrated circuits. We began by studying the simple current mirror, understanding how matched MOS transistors replicate a reference current to generate stable bias currents. The session then covered the cascode current mirror, highlighting its improved output resistance, reduced channel-length modulation effects, and enhanced current matching accuracy. Finally, we explored the wide-swing current mirror, which offers a larger output voltage swing while maintaining good current accuracy, making it suitable for low-voltage analog designs. Each current mirror topology was discussed in terms of its operating principle, advantages, limitations, and practical applications in biasing networks and analog amplifier circuits. During the laboratory session, we implemented and simulated these current mirror configurations using SPICE, analyzing their current transfer characteristics, output compliance, and overall performance. Overall, the day provided a comprehensive understanding of current mirror architectures and their significance in achieving accurate biasing and reliable analog circuit operation.

### Day 13 (June 10, Wed)
The thirteenth day introduced the *basic understanding of differential amplifiers*. We studied how differential pairs operate, focusing on input common-mode rejection, gain, and biasing. The lecture explained how differential amplifiers form the core of operational amplifiers and many analog ICs. We analyzed the small-signal model, deriving expressions for differential gain and input/output resistances. Practical examples demonstrated how differential amplifiers improve noise immunity and signal integrity. In the lab, we simulated differential pairs in SPICE, varying bias currents and transistor sizes to observe performance changes. By the end of the day, we understood why differential amplifiers are indispensable in modern analog design and how they form the foundation for complex multi-stage amplifiers.


### Day 14
The fourteenth day of training focused on the fundamentals of Integrated Circuit (IC) layout design. We were introduced to the layout design process, including device placement, interconnect routing, and the importance of adhering to fabrication design rules. The session covered key concepts such as Design Rule Check (DRC) and Layout Versus Schematic (LVS) verification, emphasizing their role in ensuring that layouts are both manufacturable and electrically consistent with the original schematic. We also discussed layout techniques such as symmetry, device matching, and careful routing to minimize parasitic effects and improve the performance and reliability of analog circuits. During the laboratory session, we gained hands-on experience with IC layout tools by creating basic MOS transistor layouts and routing interconnections while following the required design rules. Overall, the day highlighted the critical role of layout in the IC design flow and demonstrated how careful physical design directly influences circuit performance, yield, and successful fabrication.


### Day 15 (June 12, Fri)
The fifteenth day focused on **two-stage amplifier design and stability analysis**. We began by revisiting small-signal models and frequency response, then applied these concepts to multi-stage amplifiers. The lecture introduced stability metrics such as **phase margin** and **gain margin**, explaining how they determine whether an amplifier will oscillate or remain stable. We studied **Miller compensation**, a widely used technique to stabilize two-stage amplifiers, and analyzed trade-offs between bandwidth and stability. In the lab, we simulated two-stage amplifiers in SPICE, performing AC sweeps to generate Bode plots and identify poles and zeros. We also experimented with compensation capacitors, observing how design choices affect stability and performance. By the end of the day, we had a clear understanding of how to design robust amplifiers that balance high gain.
